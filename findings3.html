Findings memo, part three

Does State-Level Enforcement Line Up With State-Level Outcomes?

A first pass with official data: ICE arrest intensity by state against total payroll job growth, then 23 more state-level outcomes from BLS and CPS, kicking the tires as hard as the state cross-section allows.

Mike Konczal · Analysis run September 19, 2026 · 51 states + DC · BLS State and Area Employment (SAE) and Local Area Unemployment Statistics (LAUS) via tidyusmacro::getBLSFiles(), IPUMS CPS microdata, Deportation Data Project arrest microdata, Census Bureau Vintage 2025 population estimates

This is a geographic first pass, not a repeat of the CPS work. The first two memos (round one, round two) use CPS microdata to test native-versus-foreign-born substitution directly. CPS state cells are thin (see the caveat in that analysis), so this memo instead uses the BLS establishment survey (SAE), a near-census of state payrolls. The tradeoff: SAE cannot separate native from foreign-born workers. It answers a narrower, blunter question — did states with heavier enforcement see slower aggregate job growth, full stop — which is exactly the question a state-level enforcement-vs-outcomes chart implicitly asks.

Bottom line

No reliable relationship. States with more ICE enforcement per capita did not see slower payroll job growth over this window — if anything the point estimate runs the other way, and it is not statistically distinguishable from zero under any reasonable specification.

Read across the three specifications: the sign is not even stable-and-significant in one direction. Whatever is driving state-to-state differences in aggregate payroll growth over this window, it is not primarily enforcement intensity. That is a different conclusion from a chart built on sector-specific wage growth (construction and hospitality, the two industries with the most immigrant-intensive employment) — total nonfarm job growth mixes in every other sector, most of which have little exposure to immigration enforcement at all. The two charts are not testing the same thing, and this memo should not be read as either confirming or refuting that one.

Why DC is dropped from the trend line

DC is a genuine outlier on both axes for reasons that have nothing to do with the labor-market question being asked. Its enforcement rate is inflated by immigration-court and detention processing relative to a resident population of about 700,000, and its -5.8% payroll decline over this window is the federal-workforce-reduction story (RIFs), not an immigration-enforcement effect. Including it swings the correlation from 0.27 to 0.02 on its own — a good illustration of why a single outlier should never be allowed to silently set the answer. It stays on the chart (faint) but out of the fitted line.

Part two: kicking the tires with 23 more hypotheses

One outcome is not a research design. The rest of this memo runs every state-level outcome we could build in a reasonable pass — BLS payroll employment by sector, BLS wages, BLS/LAUS household-survey labor force statistics, and (new here) CPS microdata aggregated to the state level — against the same enforcement measure, arrests per 100,000 residents. The goal is breadth, not depth: find out which of these 23 correlations are large enough to be worth a real research design later, and which are noise. Every one of them is a simple bivariate Pearson correlation, DC excluded, no controls. Nothing here should be read as more than a screen.

What stands out

The single strongest correlation in the whole scan is a confound, not an outcome: states’ 2024 construction employment share predicts ICE enforcement intensity (r = 0.38, p = 0.007) better than anything downstream of enforcement does. Enforcement is not handed out at random across state economies — it lands hardest on states that already lean construction-heavy (Texas, Florida, Wyoming), which is exactly the kind of pre-existing difference a bivariate cross-section cannot separate from an effect of enforcement itself.

Two aggregate measures cross the p < 0.10 line in a way that is hard to read as a coherent story on its own: total private wage growth (r = 0.29, p = 0.041) rises with enforcement intensity, and so does the household-survey unemployment rate (r = 0.28, p = 0.050) — wages up, unemployment up, in the same states. Total nonfarm payroll job growth is positive too (r = 0.27, p = 0.057, repeating the Part One result). Read together this does not describe either a tightening labor market (wages up, unemployment should fall) or a slack one (unemployment up, wages should not accelerate) — it looks much more like these high-enforcement states (Texas, Florida, Arizona) were simply growing faster on every margin, enforcement included, for reasons upstream of enforcement. That is a composition story, not a causal one in either direction.

The one result that lines up with the theory’s own mechanism, for what a thin CPS state cell is worth: foreign-born prime-age employment-population ratio fell more in higher-enforcement states (r = -0.24, p = 0.089). That is short of conventional significance and should not be leaned on, but it is the correlation you would most want to see if enforcement is actually removing immigrant workers from state labor markets, and it is the right sign.

What does not replicate

The sectors that motivate the whole enforcement-and-wages argument — construction and leisure and hospitality, the two industries with the most immigrant-intensive employment — show essentially nothing here. Construction payroll job growth (r = 0.10), leisure and hospitality job growth (r = 0.02), leisure and hospitality wage growth (r = 0.02), and construction wage growth (r = -0.01) are all statistically and substantively zero. Using official BLS state-level data, sector-specific wage growth in exactly the industries a reference chart on this topic highlighted shows no relationship with state enforcement intensity at all in this window. Two methodological differences could explain part of the gap and are flagged rather than papered over: BLS does not publish a seasonally adjusted state-industry wage series, so the wage figures here are a same-month year-over-year change (Aug 2025 to Aug 2026) rather than the cumulative Jan 2025-to-window-end change used for employment and enforcement, and the construction wage series is suppressed for several states (n = 43 instead of 50). Neither of those explains away a flat correlation, but both mean this is not a clean apples-to-apples re-test of that chart’s specification.

Native-born CPS outcomes are also flat: prime-age EPOP (r = -0.10, p = 0.498) and unemployment rate (r = 0.12, p = 0.395) show no state-level relationship with enforcement intensity in either direction. That is consistent with round one’s national finding of no native benefit — it is also consistent with this cross-section simply being underpowered to detect it, since CPS state cells are thin and this design has no placebo period the way the national occupational dose-response test does.

On the multiple-comparisons problem. 5 of 23 hypotheses cross p < 0.10. Under a pure null with independent tests, chance alone would produce about 2.3. 5 is somewhat more than that, but these tests are not independent — total nonfarm job growth, the unemployment rate, and total private wages are all different measurements of the same underlying state economic conditions, so this is not 23 independent shots on goal. The honest reading is a mild, correlated signal that a handful of fast-growing, high-enforcement states (Texas above all) are pulling several of these correlations in the same direction, not 5 independently corroborating findings.

What this does not establish

This is a bivariate cross-section with about fifty observations and no controls for industry mix, pre-existing growth trends, state fiscal policy, tariff exposure, or the dozens of other things that move state payrolls. A null correlation here does not mean enforcement has no local labor-market effect — it means the effect, if any, is not visible in aggregate state payrolls once you average across every industry, including the many with negligible immigrant employment. It is also entirely consistent with two effects canceling: a negative supply/demand shock in immigrant-heavy sectors (construction, hospitality, agriculture) offset by growth elsewhere, which is exactly the kind of thing sector-specific data would show and this aggregate cut cannot. That is the natural next step, not a contradiction of it.

Arrests are administrative arrests, not deportations or removals; the two diverge (an arrest can end in release, an ongoing court case, or removal). ICE’s own state field is about 2% missing even after the Deportation Data Project’s backfill and those records are dropped, not imputed.

Natural next steps, not yet run: (1) push to the metro/city level, where tidyusmacro::getBLSFiles("sae", ...) carries area-level series for every metropolitan area BLS publishes, not just state totals — construction and hospitality are far more geographically concentrated within a state than the statewide total, so a metro cut has real power a state cut does not; (2) an event-study window around specific, dated enforcement surges (documented ICE operations in named cities) instead of a full-window average, which would sharpen identification considerably over a static cross-section; (3) a multivariate version of this scan — regress each outcome on enforcement intensity controlling for the confounds this scan surfaced (construction employment share, foreign-born population share, 2024 growth trend) instead of reporting bivariate correlations one at a time; (4) extend the CPS side with the occupational/industry dose-response design from round one (H4/H5) crossed with state, rather than a single pre/post state average.

Sources and method notes

  • ICE arrests: Deportation Data Project (UC Berkeley Law / UCLA Center for Immigration Law and Policy), FOIA-litigated ICE microdata, processed release covering 2022-10-01 through 2026-08-06, pulled from github.com/deportationdata/ice/data/arrests-latest.parquet on 2026-09-19. State is apprehension_state_filled_in (their own backfilled field); rows flagged duplicate_drop_row are dropped per their guidance. Window: arrests dated 2025-01-01 through 2026-07-31.
  • State payroll employment: BLS State and Area Employment, Hours, and Earnings (SAE), total nonfarm, seasonally adjusted, statewide (area_code == "00000"), pulled via tidyusmacro::getBLSFiles("sae", email). Growth is the simple percent change from the January 2025 level to the July 2026 level for each state; no annualization.
  • Population: Census Bureau Vintage 2025 state population estimates (POPESTIMATE2025, as of 2025-07-01, the midpoint of the enforcement window), from www2.census.gov/programs-surveys/popest/datasets/2020-2025/state/totals/.
  • Correlation and OLS run three ways — all 51 states + DC, DC excluded (primary), and DC and Texas both excluded — to check that the result is not an artifact of the two most extreme observations. See output/state_enforcement_vs_jobs_correlation.csv for exact figures.
  • Sector employment and wages (part two): BLS SAE, statewide, by supersector (construction, leisure and hospitality, manufacturing, mining and logging, professional and business services, government). Employment growth is seasonally adjusted, Jan 2025 to Jul 2026 level change. Wages (average hourly earnings) are not seasonally adjusted at the state-industry level, so wage growth is a same-month year-over-year change (most recent common month, Aug 2025 to Aug 2026) — a different window convention from the employment figures, flagged wherever it applies.
  • State unemployment and labor force (part two): BLS Local Area Unemployment Statistics (LAUS), statewide, seasonally adjusted, via tidyusmacro::getBLSFiles("laus", email). Household-survey analogue of the SAE payroll counts; changes are Jan 2025 to Jul 2026 level/rate changes.
  • CPS state panel (part two): IPUMS CPS basic monthly, the same extract used in round one and round two, aggregated to state × period cells. Pre period is calendar year 2024; post period is the most recent 12 available months (Sep 2025-Aug 2026; October 2025 has no household survey and is simply absent from the average, not interpolated). This reuses the state-panel design from round one’s H9 (R/08_geography.R) but regresses on the actual measured enforcement rate instead of a static pre-period foreign-born-share exposure proxy. Same caveat as H9: CPS state cells are thin, so treat every CPS correlation here as suggestive at best.
  • All 23 part-two hypotheses, exact r/CI/p and category, are in output/state_hypothesis_scan.csv; the merged state-level panel behind them is in data/state_hypothesis_panel.csv.
  • This memo is a first pass and stands alone; it does not revise the conclusions of round one or round two, which test a different question (native-versus-foreign-born substitution in CPS microdata) with a different method.
